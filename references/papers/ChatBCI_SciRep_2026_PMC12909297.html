Abstract

P300 speller brain computer interfaces (BCIs) allow users to compose sentences by selecting target keys on a graphical user interface (GUI) through the detection of P300 component in their electroencephalogram (EEG) signals following visual stimuli. Most existing P300 speller BCIs require users to spell all or the first few initial letters of the intended word, letter by letter. Consequently, a large number of keystrokes could be required to write an intended sentence, thereby, increasing user’s time and cognitive load. There is a need for more efficient and user-friendly methods for faster, and practical sentence composition. In this work, we introduce ChatBCI, a P300 speller BCI that leverages the zero-shot learning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) to suggest words from user-spelled initial letters or predict the subsequent word(s), reducing keystrokes and accelerating sentence composition. ChatBCI retrieves word suggestions through remote queries to the GPT-3.5 API. A modified GUI, displaying GPT-3.5 word suggestions as extra keys is designed. Stepwise linear discriminant analysis (SWLDA) is used for the P300 classification. Seven subjects completed two online spelling tasks: 1) copy-spelling a self-composed sentence using ChatBCI, and 2) improvising a sentence using ChatBCI’s word suggestions. Results demonstrate that for the copy-spelling task, on average, ChatBCI outperforms letter-by-letter BCI spellers, reducing time and keystrokes by and , respectively, and increasing information transfer rate by . For the improvised sessions, ChatBCI achieves keystroke savings across subjects. Overall, ChatBCI, by employing remote LLM queries outperforms traditional spellers without requiring local model training or storage. ChatBCI's (multi-)word prediction capability paves the way for developing next-generation speller BCIs that are efficient and effective for real-time communication, specially for users with communication and motor disabilities.

Introduction

Brain computer interfaces (BCIs) utilizing electroencephalography (EEG) offer promising potential for assistive systems by enabling direct communication between the brain and external devices1–5. Among these, P300 speller BCIs6,7 have been widely developed to support text entry without motor control. These speller BCIs find applications in aiding individuals with communication and motor disabilities, such as those with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), to express their thoughts and needs.

The P300 component, a positive peak that occurs approximately 300 ms after the stimulus onset in the event-related potential (ERP), has been considered as a reliable indicator of user intent in speller BCIs8–11. In a classical P300 speller BCI, keys in the graphical user interface (GUI) are arranged in a matrix8, with each row and column in the matrix flashing repeatedly in a random sequence to generate visual stimulations. When the user focuses on a particular key, the flashing of the row and the column containing the desired key is recognized as a deviant stimulus in an oddball paradigm, evoking a P300 response12,13. Due to the low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of single-trial EEG signals, generally multiple repetitions are required, to confirm a selection.

Over the past few decades, efforts have been made to improve BCI spellers by enhancing the speed and accuracy of making P300-based key selections. These include optimizing the GUI14, enhancing flashing patterns15,16, and adjusting inter-stimulus intervals (ISIs)17–19. These efforts however, did not target improving the spelling efficiency, as selections were mostly made letter by letter, limiting the usability of speller BCIs for composing long messages due to the fatigue and cognitive load caused by the prolonged focus required to select numerous characters from the keyboard.

Incorporating natural language processing (NLP) in P300 speller BCIs has been suggested recently to take advantage of NLP’s ability to predict and suggest words based on partial input. By learning the linguistic properties of the natural language from a training corpus, statistical language models such as N-gram or probabilistic automata have been used to estimate the probabilities for the upcoming letter or word, given the partially completed text composed by the user to enhance the spelling process.

When looking at the performance results (e.g., selection accuracy, average time to complete, and ITR) across studies listed in Table 1, it is evident that incorporating NLP improves the performance of P300 speller BCIs. However, most of these implementations require extra time, software and hardware resources to create a corpus; implement, train and update language models; and run model inferences, all of which increase the deployment costs of the BCIs. Additionally, these implementations are unable to predict the next word based on the context of preceding words to further improve their spelling efficiency. Moreover, the evaluation of these speller BCIs has mostly been limited to word spelling tasks (i.e., spelling a set of randomly selected words), which rarely occur in real-life scenarios. Sentence spelling tasks, by contrast, are more representative of real-life conversations and better suited for evaluating the performance of speller BCIs in practical applications.

Recently, the development of large language models (LLMs) has enhanced the ability to perform various NLP tasks without specific customization. Transformer-based LLMs, when scaled and trained on large datasets, display new capabilities33. One example is zero-shot learning, in which LLMs can follow instructions described in natural languages (known as “prompts”) to perform versatile and highly-specialized NLP tasks such as question answering, translation, and text generation34. This allows for rapid, low-cost implementation of complex NLP tasks via remote queries to a general LLM, eliminating the need for local model deployment. A widely recognized implementation of such LLM is ChatGPT35 (e.g., GPT-3.534) with a publicly accessible application programming interface (API).

Motivated by the advanced capabilities of LLMs, here, we present ChatBCI, a P300 speller BCI that offers predictive spelling features entirely through remote GPT-3.5 queries. Leveraging the zero-shot learning capabilities of LLMs, we use a prompt template to request candidate words from the GPT-3.5 API for completing the partial sentence composed by the user, thereby, enhancing the entire spelling process. To facilitate this for the user, a new GUI is designed for the ChatBCI. As will be demonstrated, the integration of GPT-3.5 into ChatBCI not only enables word completion, but also allows for predicting the next word or “multiple” words in a sentence based on the context of the preceding words, consequently, minimizing the number of required selections to express intent, thereby, significantly accelerating the sentence composition process.

The key contributions of this paper are as follows:

  • We introduce ChatBCI, the first P300 speller BCI to incorporate LLMs (here GPT-3.5) in an online setting, to facilitate semantic understanding of user’s spelling intent, enabling (multi-)word prediction for upcoming words in context. This goes beyond prior NLP-integrated speller BCIs20–23 that only offer word completion from initial letters, thereby improving typing speed, performance, and user experience.

  • We present a modular architectural framework for integrating high performance spelling assistance (e.g., LLMs) into BCIs. Unlike prior systems25–28,32 that rely on static dictionary lookups, our design supports remote LLM deployment for context-aware (multi-)word prediction, thereby, reducing hardware requirements and deployment costs while enabling more intelligent and adaptable user interactions. The flexibility for fully local implementations is also preserved in privacy-critical scenarios.

  • We present an updated keyboard GUI that extends traditional speller interfaces by allowing users to select not only basic characters, but also word suggestions generated by the LLM. The GUI is designed to automatically communicate with the LLM using structured messages, parse its responses, and update suggestions in real time. This design enables a more interactive and customizable spelling experience tailored to individual user preferences.

  • Using a carefully designed prompt template, we fully utilize the NLP capability of GPT-3.5 to offer intelligent word suggestions. While preserving the basic word completion function achieved by previous statistical NLP models, GPT 3.5 further enables direct prediction of the upcoming word(s) in a sentence, for additional keystroke savings. GPT-3.5 intelligently switches between word completion and prediction based on the completeness level of the last word in the partially composed sentence, without requiring any modifications to the prompt template or the layout of the word suggestion panels in the GUI. In contrast, achieving the same level of functionality with traditional statistical NLP models would have required a far more complex system.

  • We employ two experiments designed to better reflect real-world communication needs. In one task, a copy-spelling task, we ask participants to self-generate target sentences to allow for individual expression. In the second task, an improvised spelling task, participants are asked to freely compose messages using ChatBCI, fully leveraging the dynamically generated suggestions.

  • Unlike prior speller BCIs20–23 that are mostly evaluated at the character level, ChatBCI and future LLM-based predictive speller BCIs require new evaluation frameworks, as multiple characters or words can be spelled with a single selection. We propose to use “keystroke analysis” to quantify predictive typing performance and introduce the keystroke savings deficit ratio (KS-DR) to assess how closely a system approaches its theoretical maximum efficiency. Additionally, we suggest new ways to analyze ITR in such systems given that the number of displayed characters changes dynamically throughout the spelling process based on the suggested words by the LLM. To our knowledge, this is the first work to establish such evaluation metrics for LLM-integrated speller BCIs.

Overall, through integration of LLMs, we foresee great potentials in ChatBCI for improving the efficiency and user experience of P300 spellers, without introducing burdens of training, storing, or running language models locally. ChatBCI therefore, represents a new generation of P300 speller BCIs that enhances spelling experiences for the users, offering potentials for developing efficient communication tools in assistive technologies. This work can serve as a basis for developing and evaluating next-generation intelligent and efficient BCI communication systems.

Methods

Fig. 1 provides an overview of the proposed ChatBCI, which consists of a stimulation computer, a recording computer, and an EEG recording system. The stimulation computer displays the LLM-integrated GUI and transmits stimulus codes, indicating the sequence of row and column flashes on the GUI, to the recording computer. The recording computer records and processes the acquired EEG signals, and infers the user’s selected key by matching the detected P300 ERP with the flashing pattern. For EEG acquisition, we used Brain Products36. During the spelling task, the stimulation computer sends hardware triggers via the parallel port to the BrainProducts USB 2 Adapter (BUA), which relays them to the recording computer via serial connection. These markers are embedded directly into the EEG data stream, ensuring precise temporal alignment between visual stimuli and EEG recordings. This setup follows the official configuration recommended by BrainProducts whose synchronization latency has been reported below 1 ms37. Access to EEG data is facilitated through BrainProducts’ Remote Data Access (RDA) Software Development Kit (SDK), using a custom-modified client for low-latency streaming. Once a P300 response is detected, the decoded key is then sent back to the stimulation computer via a TCP socket for immediate GUI update, and remote LLM queries are conducted to retrieve and update word suggestions displayed on the GUI based on the newly-selected key. Python was used for EEG signal processing, feature extraction and SWLDA, using standard libraries including NumPy, Pandas, and Scikit-learn38,39, and interfaces with BrainVision Recorder through the RDA library for real-time acquisition. All procedures were performed in accordance with Rutgers University Institutional Review Board (IRB).

In what follows, we describe the GUI, explain how LLM is integrated with the BCI speller, and discuss how key selections are determined via detection of P300 ERP.

Graphical user interface (GUI) of the ChatBCI

Fig. 2 illustrates the GUI developed for the proposed ChatBCI. The GUI has three panels. The main body of the GUI, the keyboard panel (shown within the red box), is a matrix of keys, and includes 26 alphabet keys for letters A to Z, four function keys, and two columns on the left and right sides to display the 10 suggested candidates that are provided dynamically through GPT-3.5 queries. The function keys provide basic editing functions for the user when composing sentences: ‘DW’ (delete word) removes the last word, whether complete or incomplete, from the current sentence; ‘DC’ (delete character) removes the last character; ‘Sp’ inserts a space; and ‘En’ indicates the completion of an entry. Given the layout of of the alphabet and function keys, the number of GPT-suggested candidates was limited to 10 to maintain a visually manageable user interface. The 10 suggestions were split between the left and the right columns to maintain visual symmetry. This layout supports balanced spatial attention while offering good predictive options. In the sentence panel (shown within the green box), the “Target” section displays the target sentence for copy-spelling experiments, and the “Composed” section shows the sentence composed by the user using ChatBCI in real-time. For better visual visibility, in the “Composed” section, all letters are shown in upper case, and all space characters are shown as dashes (“-”). The experiment panel (shown within the blue box) gathers information about the experiment.

Integration with large language models (LLMs)

-Predictive Spelling Through Remote GPT Query: In ChatBCI, predictive spelling suggestions are generated from the partial text spelled by the user, through remote queries to the GPT-3.5-turbo API40. Fig. 3 illustrates an example of this process, where the user has composed “I-WOULD” as part of the target sentence of “I-WOULD-LIKE-TO-HAVE-WATER” (Fig. 3a).

Given the partial text “I-WOULD”, ChatBCI first generates a list of messages for remote GPT query. The messages are formed by filling the current partial text into a manually-engineered prompt template (Fig. 3b). To ensure ChatBCI continuously provides helpful suggestions throughout the spelling process41, we utilize the role-taking functionality of the GPT-3.5-turbo API. The prompt is split into two messages, each assuming a distinct role. Specifically, the first message, in the system role, instructs GPT on the task, the number of predictive typing keys to include, and the required response format. The second message, in the user role, contains the partial text entered by the user.

After sending the query messages to GPT, ChatBCI receives a response string.

To direct GPT to return well-formatted responses with suggestions, we set the temperature parameter to 0 for maximally deterministic behavior and compliance to the formatting instructions specified in the prompt. As ChatBCI is implemented using LLM-as-a-Service, the candidate list directly reflects the model’s response to the prompt. We manually fine-tuned the prompt template (Fig. 3b), so that most response strings, from various partial inputs, contain valid suggestions that are separable using a consistent rule (Fig. 3c). Other parametric settings for API query were kept at their default values (frequency penalty=0, maximum token count=4096, top_p=1), since they are expected to have minor impact on the quality of the concise word suggestion list created for our applications. The string is then split and reformatted into individual word suggestions (Fig. 3d), and the BCI is updated with the word suggestions acquired from the current partial text input (Fig. 3e). This allows the user to directly select “LIKE”, thereby, significantly saving time and keystrokes compared to typing each letter individually. The entire process (Fig. 3) is repeated whenever a letter or word is added to or removed from the partial text, ensuring up-to-date word suggestions based on the new partial text.

-Word Completion and Word Prediction: Using the prompt template shown in Fig. 3, GPT-3.5 intelligently provides two types of predictive spelling suggestions: word completion when the last word is incomplete, and word prediction when the last word is fully typed. For example, in the text “I-WANT-TO-B” which ends with an incomplete word, GPT suggests predictions that complete the last word, such as “BE” or “BUY”. On the other hand, for a text such as “I-WOULD” where the last word is complete but the sentence is not, GPT suggests predictions for the next word, for example “LIKE” or “WANT”.

To integrate both scenarios of word completion and word prediction in ChatBCI, we designed 2 simple rules for updating the composition based on word suggestions, optimizing intuitiveness and usability:

  1. We define the last word in a partially composed text as all characters after the last space character, if there is at least one space character in the text. Otherwise, the last word is the text. For example, for the text “I”, the last word is “I”; for the text “I-WANT-TO-B”, the last word is “B”; for the text “I-WOULD”, the last word is “WOULD”; and the last word for the text “I-WOULD-” is empty.

  2. Whenever a candidate word on the GUI is selected, it always replaces the last word in the partially-composed text, and adds a space character to the end of the text. For example, the result of selecting word “BUY” for the text “I-WANT-TO-B” is “I-WANT-TO-BUY-” (word completion); and the result of selecting word “LIKE” for the text “I-WOULD-” is “I-WOULD-LIKE-” (word prediction).

Each word selected from the suggestions automatically adds a space to the end of the text and triggers a new GPT query to update the suggestions based on the current text with the newly added word. This allows users to chain new words with just one key selection per word, thereby, significantly reducing keystrokes and speeding up the sentence composition process. If no word suggestion matches the user’s intent, the user can simply type the next letter of the intended word using the alphabet keys. This action automatically triggers a new GPT query with the updated partial input, providing additional context to the GPT and increasing the likelihood that the desired word appears in subsequent suggestions. Thus, the proposed ChatBCI can continuously provide flexible, dynamic predictive spelling suggestions, offering an effective means for composing long sentences.

P300 detection and target key recognition

To achieve P300-based spelling, the P300 component should be first detected from the acquired EEG signals, and then the corresponding target key needs to be identified. Determining the presence or absence of a P300 evoked potential from EEG signals can be treated as a binary classification problem, whereas target key recognition is a score-based selection process involving 40 candidate options, corresponding to the grid keyboard (see Fig. 2). Here, we describe the algorithms used for these two tasks.

-P300 Detection: We considered the stepwise linear discriminant analysis (SWLDA) classifier for detecting the P300 evoked potential from the EEG signals. SWLDA has shown great results in prior P300 speller BCIs10,20,21,23–27,29,42,43, making it a good candidate for ChatBCI.

SWLDA utilizes a stepwise feature selection process, iteratively adding or removing features based on their statistical significance and each feature’s individual contribution to the model. This process involves ordinary least squares (OLS) regression for evaluating features. The feature selection begins with forward regression, where OLS estimates model parameters and assesses the significance of each feature. The feature with the lowest p-value is added to the model first. In the forward selection phase, additional features demonstrating a significant and unique contribution to the variance (here with p-values less than 0.1) are included in the model. The subsequent backward elimination phase evaluates whether each included feature continues to contribute significantly, removing those with p-values greater than 0.25. This iterative process of forward selection and backward elimination continues until no further features meet the inclusion or exclusion criteria. The details of the stepwise feature selection algorithm utilized in the SWLDA classifier are provided in the Appendix (Algorithm 1). The thresholds in this algorithm for including () or discarding () features were empirically and conservatively selected, to ensure robust selection of features across all subjects, to accommodate for individual variability due to factors such as differences in head size and scalp conductance. Features selected by the algorithm are used to calculate the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score to differentiate between the two classes, corresponding to the presence or absence of P300. We used the LinearDiscriminantAnalysis class implemented in scikit-learn version 1.3.144, with the default singular value decomposition (SVD) solver.

Due to low SNR, we required 8 repetitions to recognize the target key. Each repetition involved 13 flashes, each corresponding to one of the 13 stimulus codes.

Evaluation metrics

The performance of the proposed ChatBCI was evaluated using a series of metrics. The accuracy was assessed using two measures of “Selection Accuracy” and “Character Accuracy”. The speed was assessed using the measure of “Time to Complete”. The performance was also evaluated using “Information Transfer Rate (ITR)” which takes into account both speed and accuracy. The spelling efficiency was evaluated based on a few measures involving “keystroke” analysis. In what follows, we describe these metrics.

-Time to Complete: This metric represents the time that it takes to complete writing the intended sentence using the speller, accounting for all delays (e.g., system latency), and is calculated as the difference between the task’s start and end times based on actual log data. A shorter completion time suggests a faster speller.

-Selection Accuracy: Selection accuracy is calculated as the ratio of correct selections to the total number of selections used to type the sentence. High selection accuracy indicates that fewer attempts were needed for correct spelling.

-Character Accuracy: This metric is calculated as the ratio of correctly-typed characters to the total number of characters in the sentence. A high character accuracy for a BCI suggests its better capability to accurately capture the user’s intent. This metric is calculated only for the copy-spelling tasks, as the target sentence is known.

-Character Rate: This metric represents the number of characters correctly spelled per unit time. A high character rate reflects faster and more accurate spelling performance by the BCI. Note that for the copy-spelling tasks, the character rate is computed using the total time to complete the task, which includes corrections, re-selections, and system latency, possibly leading to an underestimation of the ideal performance.

Experiments

Participants and experimental setup

Seven healthy and right-handed volunteers (four males, three females, ages ranging from 18 to 50) were recruited for this study. All had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. All participants were native or proficient English speakers who used English in daily communications. Each participant was given sufficient time to fully understand the GUI and the requirements of the spelling tasks. Only one subject had prior experience with speller BCIs. All experimental protocols were approved by the Rutgers University IRB. Written informed consent forms were obtained from each participant prior to the experiments.

The experiment was conducted in a dimly-lit and quiet room (Fig. 4). Subjects were positioned approximately 60 cm from the computer monitor. EEG signals were acquired using a 32-channel EEG system (Brain Products, actiCAP electrode cap and digitized by the BrainAmp series36, with real-time streaming enabled via BrainVision Recorder51), at a sampling rate of 250 Hz. Participants were instructed to minimize movements during the experiment.

Sessions

Each subject participated in five sessions: a calibration (training) session, a validation session, and three online sessions. The online sessions included two sessions of copy-spelling a self-created sentence (one session is done letter-by-letter used as control, and one session is done with ChatBCI), and one improvised session with words suggested by ChatBCI. Below we describe the details of these sessions.

-Calibration Session: In this session, a total of 20 keys were selected as calibration keys, ensuring a balanced distribution across the keyboard (Fig. 2). The selected keys were “A”, “En”, “B”, “Sp”, “E”, “Z”, “F”, “Y”, “M”, “R”, “H”, “W”, “K”, “T”, “I”, “V”, “U”, “J”, “N”, “Q”.

Each subject was instructed to focus on the key highlighted in green, which remained green for 2 s. Then, all rows and columns on the GUI flashed in yellow to evoke a P300 response. This process was repeated five times for each calibration key, and continued until all 20 calibration keys were presented to the subject. After the calibration session, a personalized SWLDA model was trained and used in all subsequent sessions to detect key selections for the subject.

-Validation Session: The validation session assesses the usability of the personalized model and helps the user become familiar with focusing on keys to make selections. A maximum of 10 trials was allowed for each subject. During each trial, one of the first 10 calibration keys lit up in green, indicating the key the subject should select. Then, the keys on GUI started flashing. After 8 rounds of repeated flashes, the system determined the key selected by the user and checked whether it matches the target key for that trial. The subject must make 3 consecutive correct selections within 10 trials to pass the validation session, otherwise, the calibration and validation sessions must be repeated. All seven participants in the study passed the validation session on their first attempt.

-Online Session 1: Copy-Spelling Task using ChatBCI: This session was a copy-spelling task, with the sentence created by the subjects. Each subject was presented with four images of “Apple”, “Water”, “Restroom”, and “Phone”, without any descriptions or labels (Fig. 5). We selected images representing typical daily activities (e.g., eating, drinking, restroom, communication) as representatives of realistic everyday communication needs. This did not influence the prediction capability of the LLM, as no constraint was imposed on the prompt to limit suggestions to daily activities. Prior to the experiment, subjects were asked to select one image and compose a coherent sentence relevant to the selected image, excluding punctuation. This sentence was used as their target sentence, which was then entered into the “Target” block of the GUI, as the reference sentence (Fig. 2). Subjects were then asked to copy-spell the target sentence using ChatBCI, and were encouraged to employ the GPT-based word suggestions shown on the GUI during this process. Subjects were instructed to correct mistakes, if they occurred, using as few key selections as possible by utilizing the “DC” (Delete Character) and “DW” (Delete Word) function keys.

-Online Session 2: Copy-Spelling Task, Letter by Letter: To establish a control case, after completing the copy-spelling task, subjects were asked to write the same target sentence, letter by letter, using only the alphabet and function keys. In this scenario, although the same GUI was used, only the alphabet and the function keys remained visible. The GPT query functionality was turned off, and the 10 candidate word keys were kept hidden.

-Online Session: Improvising using ChatBCI: This session involved improvising a sentence using ChatBCI. Unlike the copy-spelling task sessions that required subjects to copy-spell pre-determined sentences, the improvised session was designed to leverage the language generation capabilities of ChatGPT, to provide naturalistic context-aware spelling suggestions to the user. To standardize the initial condition across participants, to some extent, all subjects were instructed to begin their sentences with the same letter. The first word of the improvised sentence by the first subject was “His”, therefore, the remaining subjects were asked to begin their sentences with the letter ‘H’. Subjects were encouraged to avoid typing words letter by letter, and instead, utilize as many suggested words by ChatBCI as possible. While subjects were allowed to complete sentences without corrections, they were asked afterwards to report the number of incorrect selections to assess selection accuracy.

EEG signal processing

The acquired EEG signals were filtered using a bandpass filter (0.5-30 Hz) to minimize noise and artifacts. Signals from 16 channels (Fz, Cz, Pz, Oz, P3, P4, P7, P8, FC1, FC2, CP1, CP2, C3, C4, O1, O2) were selected for analysis. The reference and ground electrodes were placed at FCz and AFz, respectively. Data from each channel in the interval of 0 ms to 700 ms post-stimulus, following each flash, was extracted, resulting in a feature matrix with the dimension of per flash. To reduce the feature dimension, the moving average technique with an average window and decimation factor of 12 for each channel was applied, resulting in a feature vector length of 240 ( ) per flash. Discussions

To the best of our knowledge, ChatBCI is the first demonstration of a P300 speller BCI to effectively and efficiently incorporate LLMs (here GPT) in online sessions, and leverage their predictive power and contextual understanding to enhance the speller’s speed and performance. Additionally, by offering three typing features (word prediction, word completion, and letter-by-letter), the GUI in ChatBCI allows users to employ the speller based on their personal preferences when composing and typing sentences. As seen from the performance results, compared to a traditional letter-by-letter P300 speller, ChatBCI offers much enhanced performance, including faster communication speeds, as measured by shorter time to complete writing sentences, higher selection accuracy, and improved information transfer rates. While we did not have a survey to assess user’s experience, these improvements are expected to reduce fatigue and enhance usability when typing sentences compared to traditional letter-by-letter spellers, making ChatBCI a desirable assistive technology candidate for individuals with motor and communication disabilities.

Another contribution of this paper is the introduction of keystroke analysis to assess the predictive typing capability of speller BCIs. Typically, the performance of P300 speller BCIs is assessed using character-level metrics such as written symbol rate (WSR)52 and BCI-utility53. However, the integration of LLMs into speller BCIs introduces new capabilities, such as real-time user-driven interactions where a single selection can result in the insertion or deletion of multiple characters, that could make these metrics not directly applicable, thereby, necessitating the development of additional metrics for performance evaluation. Here, we proposed to employ keystroke analysis to specifically assess the predictive typing capability of speller BCIs equipped with word prediction functionality. We also introduced a new metric, the keystroke savings deficit ratio (KS-DR), to evaluate how far a speller BCI with predictive features is from achieving its theoretical maximum keystroke savings limit. This metric can set targets for designing efficient speller BCIs. Moreover, the predictive typing features and the new keyboard GUI, which includes word-level suggestions as selectable keys, necessitate revisiting the usual way of calculating the information transfer rate. Here, for ITR calculations, we considered two scenarios to find a lower bound (-L) and an estimate of the realistic value of ITR (-R) of ChatBCI. Future work may explore adapting other existing metrics to account for the predictive interactions and correction mechanisms specific to LLM-integrated BCIs.

As shown in Table 2, ChatBCI increased typing speed by a factor of 2.6 (from 0.94 to 2.49 char./min) while also improving accuracy. In clinical settings, this gain may reduce communication time and user fatigue, enhancing system applicability. Usability benefits are further supported by Subjects S04 and S05, who completed the copy-spelling task with ChatBCI but failed with the letter-by-letter control. Note that this result is obtained in an online setting, with system latencies and correction time included. Furthermore, since our goal was to assess the efficiency gained from integrating LLMs into the speller, we did not apply other optimization approaches (e.g., optimizing flashing paradigms or the GUI). Accordingly, this performance could be further improved by combining ChatBCI with these complementary techniques.

Additionally, we evaluated the performance of ChatBCI using a novel experiment of asking subjects to improvise sentences. Traditionally, speller BCIs have been evaluated by asking users to copy-spell pre-determined sentences. However, by having subjects improvise, we were able to assess the speller’s applicability in a more realistic scenario, and it’s effectiveness in handling spontaneous, user-generated contents. Furthermore, compared to the copy-spelling task, improvisation may better resemble everyday communication, where the intended message can be conveyed using different wordings, but what matters is that the message is effectively understood rather than expressed with exact words.

-Comparison with Prior Work: Our results show that integrating an LLM model into BCI spellers (ChatBCI) improves typing speed, ITR, and completion time. This was demonstrated in the copy-spelling task, where the same subjects spelled identical sentences using either ChatBCI or a letter-by-letter method (Tables 2 to 4). Additionally, some subjects (S04 and S05) were unable to complete the spelling task in letter-by-letter mode but succeeded with ChatBCI, highlighting its usability advantages.

In terms of the speed, ChatBCI when WC and WP features are fully utilized (i.e., in the improvised task), achieves the shortest task completion time: an average of 7.13 minutes for typing an average of 62 characters. As for selection accuracy and character accuracy, ChatBCI offers comparable or better performance in comparison to prior work, suggesting that the use of predictive features in the speller, can aid users to have less errors and mistakes.

In terms of information transfer rate, the best results among prior belong to24 and25. However, it is worth noting that unlike ChatBCI, these two studies incorporated additional optimization features to enhance the ITR metric in their speller systems. In24,25, a dynamic stopping algorithm was used to reduce the number of flashes, and25 employed an optimized flashing paradigm. Comparatively, ChatBCI, in the improvised task, when its WC and WP features are utilized, without incorporating any optimization techniques, achieves 62.56 and 73.95 bits/min for the average lower bound (-L) and an estimate of the realistic value (-R) of the ITR, respectively, across subjects.

Although we did not perform a direct comparison with prior predictive spelling-based speller BCIs, our keystroke savings analysis suggests that ChatBCI likely outperforms these approaches. Such systems typically rely on static dictionaries and provide only word completion, in which KS is inherently limited by a theoretical upper bound (KS-). In contrast, ChatBCI exceeds this limit due to its ability to offer context-driven word prediction. These findings suggest improved efficiency of LLM-integrated speller BCIs relative to conventional NLP-based approaches.

-API Cost Analysis: According to the OpenAI usage profile, on average, the API cost of GPT-enabled experiments in this study was 4291 input tokens and 1247 output tokens per sentence, respectively, or 527 input tokens and 153 output tokens per word, respectively. These convert to $0.40 per 100 sentences, or $0.05 per 100 words, according to the pricing of GPT-3.5-Turbo as of June 2025.

-Societal Impacts: ChatBCI is a modular speller BCI that offloads language-related tasks to cloud-based LLMs, enabling word completion and prediction via remote LLM API queries. This eliminates the need for costly local computation, requiring only internet access, reducing cost and lowering the entry barrier to accessible assistive BCIs. Meanwhile, transmitting patient information or communications to remote servers poses risks of personal data leakage and raises privacy concerns. Additionally, reliance on commercial LLMs introduces uncertainties, such as model changes, service retirement, internet reliability, and cost fluctuations. These risks may be limited for simple word completion, but become more problematic with sentence-level generation, where hallucinations or contextually inappropriate outputs can impair communication. Therefore, the choice between cloud-based and locally deployed BCIs should be based on the intended application.

-Limitations and Future Work: This work had some limitations. We discuss them here and suggest directions for future work.

First, ChatBCI was tested on a limited number of healthy participants () without counterbalancing the order of 3 online sessions. While this number is close to that used in other speller BCIs which focus on system development28,29,32, larger and more diverse participant groups, specially individuals who are in need of these systems, are needed to better assess generalizability, clinical applicability, and order effects of sessions.

Second, a formal user experience survey (e.g., NASA-TLX and VAS-fatigue54) can be incorporated in future studies to better assess usability, user satisfaction, cognitive load, fatigue, and other practical challenges. In this study, although surveys were not included, some aspects of usability could still be inferred from participants’ performance. For example, two subjects were unable to complete the spelling task in the letter-by-letter mode but succeeded using ChatBCI, suggesting improved usability with GPT-based assistance. To build on these findings, future work should include usability assessments and qualitative feedback to better evaluate user experience and guide improvements.

Third, in this work our focus was on assessing the impact of integrating LLM-based predictions on the spelling performance. Improvements can be made in terms of optimizing other parts of the system, which may further enhance speed, accuracy, and user experience in future versions of ChatBCI. For example, we fixed the number of repetitions across all experiments and their respective control versions to reduce the number of variables contributing to outcomes. Future work may include incorporating paradigm-oriented optimization techniques such as dynamic stopping20,24,29, optimized flash paradigms25, and GUI adjustments55 into ChatBCI. Furthermore, we adopted SWLDA due to its simplicity and common usage in P300 speller BCIs. Exploring alternative classifiers in future work may offer opportunities to further improve the performance of the system. Our calibration session lasted about 8 minutes, with no participant expressing concerns about its duration. While calibration is standard in speller BCIs due to individual variability, future work may explore methods for minimizing or eliminating calibration to further improve usability.

Fourth, the EEG system used in our study provided adequate signal quality during sessions. However, like any other EEG-based system, long-term degradation may occur. With hardware-based research in improving electrode stability, signal acquisition robustness, and user comfort, such challenges can potentially be mitigated in future implementations of EEG systems.

Fifth, in terms of spelling performance and prediction events, our results showed that in a few cases, the user did not select the suggested word by ChatBCI displayed in the GUI, and continued with selecting the next letter, or the 10 suggestions generated by GPT did not match the user’s intended word, likely due to contextual limitations (e.g., short prefixes like “I”). Future improvements could include better user training and fine-tuning the LLM for the specific applications to enhance prediction relevance.

Interestingly, while in this work, multi-word suggestions were not a primary design objective of ChatBCI, our results indicate they can improve spelling efficiency by enabling phrase-level selection in a single step. To further improve the typing speed, future versions of ChatBCI could explore strategies to optimize the generation, display, and selection of multi-word suggestions. This includes refining prompt engineering to encourage phrase-level predictions and adapting the GUI to support longer suggestions during real-time spelling tasks. Additionally, we plan to explore ChatBCI variants with locally-deployed LLMs to enable offline operation when issues such as privacy, consistency, or internet access are of concern. Furthermore, to support comprehensive evaluation, we focused on full-sentence spelling tasks reflecting broader communication needs. However, the system’s word completion feature may also aid keyword based intent prediction, which could be of interest to individuals relying on alternative communication methods and can be evaluated in the future.

Finally, we would like to note that the name ChatBCI was initially inspired by the integration of chat-optimized LLMs (e.g., GPT-3.5) to enable predictive communication in speller BCIs (similar to how chat applications anticipate user intent during conversation). Our results demonstrated that by incorporating LLMs, ChatBCI offers advancements in spelling efficiency, typing speed, user experience, and overall performance, compared to existing spellers. Beyond these improvements, the system also holds strong potential as a step toward future conversational BCIs. Its envisioned integration with communication platforms (e.g., text messaging, email, or chat with caregivers) could enable a “chat-style” communication experience for individuals who rely on assistive technologies for communication.

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced ChatBCI, the first demonstration of a P300 speller BCI that incorporates LLMs (here GPT-3.5) to improve typing speed and performance in online sessions, by offering predictive typing features. By enabling (multi-)word prediction capability through the integration of LLMs, ChatBCI reduces the need for letter-by-letter selection, reducing keystrokes, task time, and consequently user’s cognitive load. ChatBCI also features a new keyboard GUI that offers users with three typing options (word prediction, word completion, and letter-by-letter) allowing them to use the speller according to their personal preferences when composing sentences. To assess ChatBCI’s real-world applicability, we evaluated it not only through a traditional copy-spelling task but also through a new task, where users improvised sentences. This approach can assess speller’s performance in practical, spontaneous communication scenarios. Additionally, we introduced keystroke analysis and a new metric, the keystroke savings deficit ratio (KS-DR), to quantify speller’s predictive typing performance. Results from two online tasks demonstrate that ChatBCI significantly outperforms traditional letter-by-letter P300 spellers. For the improvised task, ChatBCI achieved keystroke savings, averaged across subjects. With its multi-word prediction feature, ChatBCI can surpass the theoretical keystroke savings limit, underscoring the potential of LLM-integrated speller BCIs for practical use. Our results on healthy subjects suggest that ChatBCI shows promise in positioning it as a promising assistive technology, however, further validation in the target population is needed to confirm its clinical applicability.
